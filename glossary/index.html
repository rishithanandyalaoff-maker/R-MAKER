Rules as of tax year 2026

Glossary

65 terms used across the lessons and tools.

  • Gross pay

    Pay before deductions

    Your salary or wages before taxes, health premiums, or retirement contributions are taken out.

  • Take-home pay

    What hits your bank

    Money left after taxes and paycheck deductions. Also called net pay.

  • Marginal tax rate

    Tax on your next dollar

    The tax rate that applies to your next dollar of taxable income. It is not the rate you pay on every dollar.

  • Effective tax rate

    Average tax rate

    Total tax divided by income. Usually lower than your marginal rate because only the top slice is taxed at the top bracket.

  • Standard deduction

    Automatic tax reduction

    A fixed dollar amount you subtract from income before figuring federal tax, if you do not itemize deductions.

  • Tax bracket

    Rate band for income

    A range of taxable income taxed at one federal rate. Higher income fills higher brackets; lower brackets still apply to earlier dollars.

  • FICA

    Social Security + Medicare

    Payroll taxes for Social Security and Medicare. Often shown separately from federal income tax.

  • Social Security tax

    6.2% up to a wage base

    Employee Social Security tax is a percent of wages up to an annual wage base. Some F-1/OPT nonresidents are exempt.

  • Medicare tax

    1.45% (+ extra at high income)

    Employee Medicare tax is usually 1.45% of wages, with an extra 0.9% above a high-income threshold.

  • 401(k)

    Workplace retirement account

    A retirement plan opened through your employer. Money can go in before tax (traditional) or after tax (Roth), up to an annual employee limit.

  • IRA

    Individual retirement account

    A retirement account you open yourself at a brokerage. Separate yearly limit from a 401(k).

  • Roth

    Taxed now, tax-free later

    Contributions are after-tax. Qualified withdrawals of growth later can be tax-free. Useful when your tax rate today is low.

  • Traditional

    Tax break now, taxed later

    Contributions may reduce taxable income now. Withdrawals in retirement are generally taxed as ordinary income.

  • Backdoor Roth

    Roth path when income is high

    Contribute to a traditional IRA (nondeductible) then convert to Roth. Works cleanly if your traditional IRA balance stays near $0 (pro-rata rule).

  • Pro-rata rule

    Taxes conversions by mix

    If you have pretax traditional IRA money, Roth conversions are taxed in proportion to pretax vs after-tax IRA balances.

  • Employer match

    Free money if you contribute

    Your employer adds money only if you contribute enough. Contribute at least enough to get the full match.

  • Automatic employer contribution

    Free money even if you put in $0

    Some employers contribute a percent of pay without requiring you to contribute. Confirm with HR — it is not always a match.

  • Vesting

    When employer money is yours

    A schedule for when employer contributions become fully yours if you leave the company.

  • HSA

    Health savings account

    A triple-tax-advantaged account paired with certain high-deductible health plans. Can be invested and used for qualified medical costs.

  • ETF

    Exchange-traded fund

    A fund that trades like a stock and usually holds a basket of investments, often tracking an index.

  • Index fund

    Owns the market basket

    A fund built to track a market index (for example, the total US stock market) instead of picking winners.

  • Expense ratio

    Annual fund fee

    The yearly fee a fund charges as a percent of assets. Lower is usually better for the same kind of fund.

  • Asset allocation

    Mix of stocks, bonds, cash

    How you split money across investment types. Time horizon and risk comfort guide the mix.

  • Rebalancing

    Resetting your mix

    Buying or selling to bring your portfolio back to the target allocation after markets move.

  • Dollar-cost averaging

    Investing on a schedule

    Putting money in regularly over time. Historically, lump sum often wins, but monthly investing can feel easier.

  • Capital gains

    Profit from selling investments

    Taxed differently depending on how long you held the investment. Holding over a year can qualify for long-term rates.

  • Wash-sale rule

    Blocks some loss claims

    If you sell at a loss and buy a substantially identical investment too soon, you may not deduct that loss right away. Also watch IRAs.

  • Tax-loss harvesting

    Using losses to offset gains

    Selling investments at a loss to offset taxable gains, while staying mindful of the wash-sale rule.

  • APR

    Yearly interest rate

    Annual percentage rate. Higher APR debt usually deserves faster payoff.

  • Refinance

    Replace a loan with a new one

    Taking a new loan, often at a lower rate, to replace an old one. Visa status can affect who will lend.

  • Cosigner

    Someone who shares loan responsibility

    A person who promises to repay if you cannot. Some lenders ask internationals for a US citizen or green-card cosigner.

  • Emergency fund

    Cash for surprises

    Money set aside for job loss or big unexpected costs. Often 3–6 months of expenses; closer to 6 on a visa.

  • HYSA

    High-yield savings account

    A savings account that pays a higher interest rate than a typical checking account. Good for emergency cash.

  • CD

    Certificate of deposit

    A timed deposit that usually pays more interest if you leave money alone until maturity. Early withdrawals may cost a penalty.

  • T-bill

    Short-term US Treasury bill

    A short-term government security. Often used for cash you need within months to a couple of years.

  • Compounding

    Growth on growth

    Earning returns on both your original money and earlier returns. Time is the main ingredient.

  • Financial independence number

    About 25× yearly spending

    A rough savings target so investment returns could cover living costs. Example: $40,000/year spending → about $1,000,000.

  • Savings rate

    Share of income you keep

    How much of your income you save or invest. Early on, savings rate usually matters more than investment returns.

  • Brokerage account

    Flexible taxable investing

    A normal investing account with no special retirement tax wrapper. Usually the most flexible place for money you may need before age 59½.

  • Vested balance

    What you keep if you leave

    The portion of retirement money you own outright, including your contributions and any vested employer money.

  • Rollover

    Moving retirement money

    Transferring funds from one retirement account to another, often when you change jobs. Rules matter for taxes and penalties.

  • RMD

    Required minimum distribution

    Forced withdrawals from some retirement accounts starting at a later age. Roth IRAs generally do not require them during the owner’s lifetime.

  • FBAR

    Foreign account report

    A US report for people with foreign financial accounts over a threshold (commonly $10,000 aggregate). Separate from your tax return.

  • PFIC

    Foreign mutual fund tax trap

    Many non-US mutual funds are PFICs for US tax residents and can create painful tax reporting. Prefer US-domiciled funds once you are a US tax resident.

  • Form 1040-NR

    Nonresident tax return

    The US tax return often used by nonresident aliens. Treaty benefits may apply.

  • OPT

    Optional Practical Training

    Work authorization for many F-1 students after study. Timing and FICA rules can differ from H-1B.

  • STEM OPT

    Extra OPT years for STEM

    An extension of OPT for eligible STEM degrees. Confirm details with your school’s international office.

  • Cap-gap

    Bridge to H-1B start

    A status bridge that can help some F-1/OPT workers stay authorized until an October H-1B start.

  • H-1B

    Specialty-occupation work visa

    A common work visa for specialty roles. Lottery timing and start dates matter for taxes and loan refinancing.

  • I-797

    USCIS approval notice

    A notice from USCIS that often documents petition approvals, including H-1B approvals.

  • RNOR

    India tax residency window

    Returning Non-Resident status in India that can create a temporary tax window after returning. Confirm with a cross-border tax professional.

  • Totalization agreement

    Social Security treaty

    An agreement between countries about Social Security coverage and credits. Not every country has one with the US.

  • Withholding

    Tax taken from pay

    Money your employer sends to the tax agency from each paycheck. It is an estimate, not always your final tax bill.

  • Bonus withholding

    Often 22% federal supplemental

    Many bonuses are withheld at a flat federal supplemental rate. Your eventual tax on that bonus may differ.

  • Liquidity

    How fast you can use cash

    How quickly you can turn an asset into spendable money without a big penalty or delay.

  • Risk tolerance

    How much ups and downs you can handle

    Your ability and willingness to see account values swing. Time horizon and job security matter.

  • Time horizon

    When you need the money

    How soon you will spend the money. Money needed within about two years usually stays out of stocks.

  • Diversification

    Not one bet

    Spreading investments so one company or country cannot sink your whole plan.

  • Target-date fund

    One-fund mix by year

    A fund that holds a mix of stocks and bonds and usually gets more conservative as a target year approaches.

  • Mega backdoor Roth

    After-tax 401(k) → Roth

    If your plan allows after-tax 401(k) contributions and in-plan Roth conversions or rollovers, you may move more into Roth. Ask HR.

  • Age 59½ rule

    Early withdrawal line

    Many retirement withdrawals before 59½ face income tax and an extra 10% penalty, with exceptions.

  • Roth IRA ordering rules

    Contributions come out first

    Roth IRA withdrawals are generally treated as contributions first, then conversions, then earnings — with different tax and timing rules.

  • Beneficiary

    Who inherits an account

    The person or entity named to receive an account if you die. Update these when life changes.

  • Umbrella insurance

    Extra liability cover

    A policy that adds liability protection above auto and home/renters coverage. Often later in a plan.

  • Own-occupation disability

    Pays if you cannot do your job

    Disability insurance that looks at whether you can do your own occupation, not just any job.

Educational only — not financial, tax, legal or immigration advice. Rules shown are US federal rules for tax year 2026; confirm with IRS.gov, your employer's HR, your school's international office, or a licensed professional.
