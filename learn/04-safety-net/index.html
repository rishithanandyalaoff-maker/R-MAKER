Your safety net

Learning goal: size an emergency fund and park it in the right kind of cash.

1. Why cash exists

An emergency fund is money for job loss, medical bills, or sudden moves — not for market timing. Without it, people sell investments at the worst time or put emergencies on high-rate credit cards.

2. How much

Common teaching range: 3–6 months of essential expenses. Lean toward ~6 months if your job, visa, or industry is less stable. Many people keep a small starter fund ($1,000–$2,000) before attacking high-rate debt, so one surprise bill doesn't go on a credit card.

Visa note

International grads often prefer the higher end. OPT endings, H-1B timing, and relocation costs can arrive faster than a typical domestic career path.

3. Where to keep it

High-yield savings accounts (HYSA) are the default teaching choice: liquid, FDIC-insured at member banks, and they earn more than a basic checking account. FDIC insurance covers up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. Some app-based savings accounts are insured only through partner banks — check.

CDs and T-bills can fit money you will not need for a known few months, but keep true emergencies liquid. T-bill interest is exempt from state income tax. CDs usually charge a few months of interest for early withdrawal; no-penalty CDs exist at slightly lower rates.

Example

If essentials cost $3,000/month, a 6-month fund is about $18,000. Build it after capturing any free employer match and after crushing very high-interest debt (aside from a small starter cushion).

4. Order reminder

Cash is not the first dollar forever. Free match usually comes first; then high-rate debt; then the emergency fund; then long-term investing. The Account picker tool walks that order.

Key takeaways

  • 3–6 months of essentials; ~6 if your path is uncertain.
  • HYSA for liquid emergencies; confirm FDIC coverage.
  • Cash after free match and high-rate debt, before aggressive investing.
