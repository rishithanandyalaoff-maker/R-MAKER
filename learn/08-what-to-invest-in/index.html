What to invest in

Learning goal: pick a simple, diversified default instead of stock picking.

1. Own the market cheaply

Broad index funds are the teaching default. Neutral examples (tickers change — confirm before buying): US total market (e.g. VTI), international (e.g. VXUS), whole world (e.g. VT), or a target-date fund that mixes stocks and bonds for you.

Fees matter. On $10,000, a 0.03% expense ratio costs about $3/year; a 1% fee costs about $100/year. Prefer low expense ratios.

2. Allocation = risk dial

Stocks grow more over long periods but swing hard. Bonds and cash swing less. Younger investors with long horizons often hold more stocks; money needed within a few years stays safer. A typical long-term stock split is about 70% US / ~20–30% international. Rebalance about once a year.

3. Taxes in a brokerage

Assets held over a year usually qualify for long-term capital gains rates of 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on income. The wash-sale rule can disallow a loss if you buy a “substantially identical” security within 30 days — including buying the replacement in an IRA.

4. Simple rules of thumb

  • Automate contributions every paycheck.
  • Prefer low expense ratios.
  • Ignore day-to-day market noise.
  • Do not invest emergency cash in stocks.
  • Individual stocks ≤10% of total, no single stock >5%, brokerage account only.
  • Lump sum has beaten spreading out about two-thirds of the time historically.

Watch out

Past returns are not guarantees. The 7% figure used in tools is a long-run teaching assumption, not a promise. Consulting, finance and tech employers often restrict personal trading. Read your employer's policy before buying individual stocks.

Key takeaways

  • Broad low-cost funds beat most DIY stock picking for beginners.
  • Match risk to time horizon; rebalance yearly.
  • Consistency beats cleverness.
