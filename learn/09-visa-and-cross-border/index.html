Visa and cross-border money

Learning goal: Handle taxes, accounts and big decisions as an F-1, OPT or H-1B worker — including if you leave the US.

1. Who this lesson is for

Skip if you are a US citizen or green-card holder with no foreign accounts. Read it if you are on F-1, OPT, STEM OPT, H-1B or a similar status, or if you might move abroad.

2. Your tax status as a student

  • Most F-1 students are "exempt individuals" for their first 5 calendar years in the US. During those years you are usually a nonresident alien for tax, even while working on OPT.
  • Nonresidents file Form 1040-NR, not the regular 1040. Most consumer tax software does not support it; tools such as Sprintax do, and many schools offer free access.
  • Tax treaties can lower your tax. Example: under the US–India treaty, Indian students can usually claim the standard deduction, which most nonresidents cannot. Look up your country's treaty in IRS Publication 901.
  • Your state may treat you differently from the federal rules. Check your state's instructions.

Example

Internship + signing-bonus years often produce a refund. Payroll withholds as if your salary lasts all year, and bonuses are withheld at a flat 22%. A student who earned $75,000 in a partial year may get several thousand dollars back.

3. Social Security and Medicare (FICA)

  • While you are a nonresident on F-1 or OPT, you are generally exempt from Social Security and Medicare (7.65%).
  • Tell payroll your status in your first week. If FICA was withheld by mistake, ask your employer for a refund first; if they cannot, file Form 843 with Form 8316.
  • The exemption ends when you change to H-1B (or become a tax resident). Take-home drops by about 7.65% overnight — plan for it.
  • Social Security benefits generally require about 10 years of US work. The US has "totalization agreements" with some countries that let work count in both; it has none with India.

4. The H-1B timeline

  • Registration lottery: March. Results: late March.
  • A first-time ("cap") H-1B starts October 1 at the earliest, even if approved in spring.
  • "Cap-gap" lets F-1/OPT workers keep working until October 1 if the petition was filed on time.
  • Your first H-1B year is often dual-status for tax (nonresident part of the year, resident the rest), with special elections. Use a tax preparer for that year.
  • Job loss: H-1B workers generally have a grace period of up to 60 days; OPT allows a limited number of unemployment days (90, or 150 total with STEM OPT). This is why a 6-month emergency fund matters.

5. Once you are a US tax resident

  • The US taxes your worldwide income, including interest earned in your home country.
  • File an FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) if your foreign accounts total more than $10,000 at any point in the year. Larger balances may also need Form 8938.
  • Avoid holding home-country mutual funds or ETFs. US "PFIC" rules tax them harshly and require complex filings. Home-country bank deposits are simpler.
  • Decide what to do with savings back home: keep them as a family or safety fund, or move them to the US to invest.

6. Building credit and banking

  • You may not have a credit history at first. Options: a student or secured credit card, or becoming an authorized user on a trusted person's card.
  • Pay every card in full each month. A year of history makes renting and loan refinancing easier and cheaper.
  • Many lenders (including for refinancing) require H-1B or a green card, or a US citizen/green-card cosigner.
  • Keep cash for deposits, first and last month's rent, and a flight home.

7. If you might leave the US within ~5 years (short-horizon path)

  • Still take any free employer match — but check the vesting schedule.
  • Keep more in cash and a brokerage account, and less in accounts locked until 59½.
  • Use a safer investment mix for money you will need when you move.
  • Pay down US debt before leaving; repaying a US loan from abroad is harder.
  • Staying on OPT (if your plans are firm) keeps the FICA exemption; switching to H-1B only to refinance a loan can cost more in FICA than it saves in interest. Run the refinance sketch in Lesson 5 with your own numbers.

8. If you leave the US

  • You do not have to cash out. 401(k)s and IRAs can stay invested. Use a brokerage that supports non-US residents, and keep one US bank account.
  • The 10% early-withdrawal penalty depends on your age, not where you live.
  • The US still taxes withdrawals from US retirement accounts (often with 30% withholding for nonresidents, which a treaty may reduce). Your new country may tax them too, usually with a credit for US tax.
  • Many countries, including India, do not treat Roth accounts as tax-free. Holding both traditional and Roth money spreads this risk.
  • Returning Indian residents often qualify for 2–3 years of RNOR status (resident but not ordinarily resident), during which India generally does not tax foreign income. That window can be a good time to sell or withdraw.
  • Nonresidents who own more than $60,000 of US assets (including US stocks and retirement accounts) can face US estate tax at death. Keep a will and named beneficiaries.

Visa note

Rules differ by country, treaty and personal history. Before filing, changing status, or withdrawing money across borders, talk to your school's international office and a tax professional who handles cross-border cases.

Key takeaways

  • F-1/OPT years: usually nonresident, 1040-NR, no FICA — and often a refund.
  • H-1B starts October 1 at the earliest and adds FICA.
  • Leaving the US does not force cashing out; plan withdrawals around your new country's taxes.
  • Short stay? Keep money flexible after capturing free match.

Quick check

Three questions to lock in the visa money basics.

1. On F-1 OPT in your second year in the US, do you usually pay Social Security and Medicare?
2. Your H-1B was approved in May. When can it start?
3. You move home at 34. Must you cash out your 401(k)?
