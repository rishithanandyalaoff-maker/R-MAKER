The four retirement accounts

Learning goal: tell 401(k) from IRA and traditional from Roth.

1. Employer plan vs IRA

A 401(k) is offered through work. An IRA is an account you open yourself. A 401(k) can be traditional (pre-tax) or Roth (after-tax). An IRA can also be traditional or Roth, with two income rules:

  • Traditional IRA contributions are tax-deductible only if your income is low enough. If you have a workplace plan, the 2026 deduction phases out at about $81,000–$91,000 for single filers. Above that, a traditional IRA has no upfront tax break.
  • Roth IRA contributions phase out at about $153,000–$168,000 (single) and $242,000–$252,000 (married filing jointly).

Above those limits, many people use a backdoor Roth (below).

This year's teaching limits: employee 401(k) $24,500; IRA $7,500. Confirm on IRS.gov before acting.

2. Match vs automatic contribution

A match pays only if you contribute (e.g., 50% of what you put in up to 6% of pay); an automatic contribution arrives regardless. Check the vesting schedule — unvested employer money is lost if you leave early — and whether you must be employed on the payment date.

3. Traditional vs Roth

Traditional lowers taxable income now; you pay tax later. Roth does not lower income now; qualified withdrawals later are tax-free. When today's and tomorrow's tax rates match, results can match — see the worked comparison.

On $1,000 of salary, 24% tax now and later, 7% for 30 years:

  • Traditional after tax later: $5,785
  • Roth after tax later: $5,785

When rates match, results match. Choose Roth when today's tax rate is lower than you expect in retirement (for example, a partial first year of work); traditional when today's rate is higher.

4. Backdoor Roth

Contribute to a traditional IRA (non-deductible), convert to Roth soon after. Keep your pre-tax IRA balance at $0 because of the pro-rata rule; never roll an old 401(k) into an IRA if you use the backdoor — roll it into the next employer's 401(k).

5. Mega backdoor Roth

Some 401(k) plans allow after-tax contributions beyond $24,500 plus in-plan Roth conversion. Ask HR.

6. HSA

If you have a high-deductible health plan, an HSA is tax-free in, while invested, and out for medical costs.

Key takeaways

  • 401(k) = work plan; IRA = your own (with income phase-outs).
  • Match first when available; check vesting.
  • Roth vs traditional hinges on tax rates now vs later.
